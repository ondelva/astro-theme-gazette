For three years I worked a rotating shift at a regional news desk, two weeks on days, one week on nights, a pattern the industry considered humane compared to a fixed night schedule because it let people keep a version of a normal life during the day weeks. I have come to think it was, if anything, worse, because a body that never gets to settle into either rhythm never gets to stop fighting either one.

The science of what shift work does to a person is not new, and it is not gentle. The human body runs on a circadian rhythm, an internal clock regulated primarily by a small structure in the brain that responds to light, and that clock governs far more than sleepiness: core body temperature, hormone release, digestion, even the timing of cell repair processes all follow its schedule. Night work doesn't simply ask a person to be awake at the wrong hour. It asks every internal system tuned to that clock to operate against its own instructions, repeatedly, sometimes for a working lifetime.

## The gap between adjusting and only coping

I used to believe, the way most people who haven't done shift work believe, that the body adjusts, given enough time. It doesn't, not fully, and the research on this is fairly unambiguous: even career night workers, people who have worked exclusively overnight for decades, show circadian markers that never fully invert to match their waking hours, because daylight exposure during their off hours keeps partially resetting a clock their schedule is simultaneously trying to override. What actually happens with long-term night work isn't adaptation. It's a permanent, low-grade misalignment that people learn to function within, which is a different thing entirely from resolving it.

The body doesn't learn to work nights, in other words. It learns to survive them, and calls the survival adjustment, because the alternative is admitting the fight never ends. This misalignment has consequences well documented enough that public health bodies in several countries now classify long-term night shift work as a probable contributor to a range of health outcomes, from cardiovascular disease to certain cancers, associations that remain debated in their exact mechanisms but not in their basic existence across large population studies. None of this was news to the people I worked alongside on the rotating desk. Everyone on a night shift already knows, in the way a body knows something a study only confirms, that something during those hours is costing them more than the paycheck accounts for.

## What keeps people doing it anyway

And yet the work gets done, by people who choose it, repeatedly, for reasons that have very little to do with not knowing the cost. Some take night shifts for the pay differential, which in many industries meaningfully exceeds what the disruption alone would justify on paper. Some take them because the hours genuinely suit a life built around something else: childcare during the day, a second job, a temperament that simply prefers the particular quiet of an empty building or an empty street at 3 a.m., which several people I interviewed for a piece elsewhere in this issue described with something closer to affection than complaint.

I don't think there's a clean moral to draw from any of this, no simple argument that night work should end or that everyone doing it is being exploited by it, though some clearly are, in industries where the differential doesn't come close to compensating for what's actually being asked. What I keep returning to, three years removed from that rotating schedule myself, is smaller and less resolvable: a modern city runs twenty-four hours because we have built an economy that requires it to, and every one of those hours is staffed by a body that did not evolve for the shift it's currently working. We have engineered the infrastructure to run continuously. We have not engineered the humans running it to match, and I'm not sure that gap is one better lighting or a smarter schedule can ever fully close.
