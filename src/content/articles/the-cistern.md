The cistern under my grandmother's house held about nine thousand litres, though she never spoke of it in litres. She spoke of it in weeks: how many weeks a full tank would carry the household through a dry spell, how many days a leaking gutter joint could cost her if it went unrepaired through a season of light rain. Water, in her arithmetic, was never a quantity. It was time.

I grew up visiting that house in the school holidays, in a town the municipal supply reached only in the last years of her life, and long after most of the country around it had running water on demand. The cistern sat beneath the courtyard, fed by a network of gutters and a first-flush diverter, a simple pipe fitting that let the initial, dirtiest runoff from the roof wash away before the cleaner rain that followed was allowed to enter the tank. She had installed it herself, from a diagram in a farming almanac, decades before rainwater harvesting became a phrase planners used in reports.

## A system that punished forgetting

What I remember most is not the tank itself but the discipline that surrounded it, a set of small, constant checks that had nothing to do with technology and everything to do with attention. Every morning she walked the gutter line, looking for leaves, for a bird's nest wedged in a downpipe, for the telltale dark stain that meant a joint had started to weep. A single blocked gutter, ignored for a week, could mean the difference between a full cistern after a storm and a half-empty one. The system had no automation, no sensor, no alarm. It had her.

I did not understand, as a child, that this was labor. It looked like habit, the way she'd glance up at the roofline on her way to hang washing, the way she kept a coil of wire by the back door specifically for clearing downpipes. It was only much later, filling a glass from a tap that had never once run dry in my adult life, that I understood what her attention had been standing in for: an entire municipal system's worth of engineering, redundancy, and maintenance crews, all of it compressed into one woman's daily circuit of her own roof.

## What the tap replaced, and what it didn't

The municipal line reached her street the year I turned twenty-two, and she connected to it within the month, no sentimentality about the cistern at all. She'd spent forty years managing scarcity by hand and was glad to be done with it. But she kept the tank. Kept the gutters clean out of habit. Kept, until she died, a private conviction that the municipal supply was a convenience layered on top of a system that still, underneath, worked the way hers had: collection, storage, distribution, and constant unglamorous maintenance, just performed at a scale and a distance that let the people at the end of the pipe forget it was happening at all.

That forgetting is, in a sense, the entire point of municipal infrastructure. It is built so that nobody has to walk a gutter line every morning, so that water becomes a background condition of life rather than a daily negotiation. I don't think this is a loss to mourn. A city where every household must manage its own cistern is not a city with more wisdom about water, it's a city that hasn't yet been able to afford the alternative.

But I have started walking my own building's roof access twice a year, checking the drains that feed the storm system below my flat, a habit that makes no practical difference to my water supply and that I cannot quite explain except as inheritance. My grandmother's real lesson was never about tanks or gutters. It was that the systems keeping you alive are never as automatic as they feel, and that somewhere, someone, or something, is still doing the walk she used to do, whether or not you ever see them do it.
