At 10 p.m., a forty-two-story office tower that held roughly three thousand people eleven hours earlier holds twenty-eight: a cleaning contractor's overnight crew, moving floor by floor through a building that has, for their purposes, effectively stopped being a workplace and become something closer to a large, mostly dark warehouse they have to clear out by 6 a.m.

The overnight cleaning industry runs almost entirely on a logic invisible to the people who benefit from it: office work and deep cleaning are largely incompatible activities in the same space at the same time, so the cleaning happens in the hours the work doesn't. Floor supervisor Grace Adeyemi has managed overnight crews across four buildings in the district for seven years and says the scheduling math is simple even if the execution isn't: a floor takes roughly forty minutes to clean thoroughly, vacuuming, emptying and relining waste bins, wiping surfaces, restocking supplies, and with a standard crew size on a forty-two-story building, that arithmetic only works if the building is actually vacated on schedule.

## The work that only happens when nobody's watching

Beyond the nightly routine, buildings this size schedule deeper maintenance tasks specifically for overnight windows, work that would be disruptive or simply impossible during business hours: carpet extraction cleaning, which requires machinery too loud and too wet-footed for an occupied floor; stripping and rewaxing hard floor surfaces in lobbies and common areas, a multi-hour process requiring the floor to be fully closed off while it cures; and window washing on lower floors, scheduled overnight in this building specifically because the property manager found daytime washing distracted workers seated near the glass.

"People email me sometimes asking why a floor smells like fresh wax at nine in the morning," said building operations manager Colin Harte. "It's because at eleven the night before, that floor was a construction site, basically, machinery everywhere, and by six it had to look like nothing happened at all. That's the actual job of this crew, making four hours of heavy work invisible by morning."

## An industry mostly staffed by people who don't work in the buildings they clean

The cleaning contractor employs 340 people across the district's commercial towers, the large majority on overnight shifts, and turnover in the industry runs considerably higher than in daytime office work itself, a pattern Adeyemi attributes less to the work's physical demands than to its social cost. Overnight cleaners, unlike the office workers whose desks they clean, rarely interact with the people whose space they maintain at all; a note left on a desk, a coffee cup washed and returned, are often the only trace of the relationship in either direction.

Pay for overnight commercial cleaning in the district sits modestly above minimum wage on average, with a shift differential for night hours that several workers interviewed for this piece described as insufficient to offset the toll of a permanently inverted schedule, a complaint echoed in recent contract negotiations between cleaning unions and building management associations in the district, which resulted in a phased wage increase taking effect over the next two years.

Adeyemi's crew finishes the forty-two floors by 5:40 most nights, with twenty minutes held in reserve for anything unexpected, a spill, a locked door, a floor left in worse condition than usual. By six, the building's lobby lights shift to their daytime setting on an automated timer, and the first office workers of the morning begin arriving to a building that looks, to them, as though it simply never needed anyone at all.
