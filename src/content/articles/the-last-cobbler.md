When Ezekiel Moyo took over his father's shoe repair shop on Fen Street in 1991, there were three other cobblers within four hundred meters of his door. The last of them, a shop that had specialized in orthopedic modifications for two decades, closed in 2019, leaving Moyo's shop the only one left on a street that used to be known locally for having more shoemakers per block than any other in the district.

"It's not that people stopped wearing out shoes," Moyo says, resoling a pair of leather boots on the workbench that used to be his father's. "It's that they stopped believing shoes could be fixed. A lot of what's sold now isn't built to be repaired in the first place, glued rather than stitched, soles bonded in a way that doesn't come apart cleanly. You can still fix almost anything, but sometimes fixing it costs more in labor than the shoe cost new, and I have to tell people that honestly, even though it's not good for my business to say it."

The trade itself hasn't changed as much as the shoes have. Moyo still resoles using a Blake stitch machine older than he is, a mechanism that sews a sole directly to the shoe's insole through a single line of stitching visible from inside the shoe, a construction method that allows a sole to be replaced multiple times over a shoe's life without damaging the upper. It's the same construction found in well-made dress shoes going back over a century, and it remains, he says, the gold standard for a shoe meant to last. The difference is how few pairs arrive at his counter built that way anymore.

What sustains the shop now is not volume but relationship. Moyo estimates roughly sixty percent of his repeat customers have been coming to him for over a decade, some for the full thirty years he's run the place, bringing in the same handful of well-made pairs, a wedding pair of oxfords, a father's old boots, a pair bought specifically because it was resoleable, again and again over the years rather than replacing them. He knows many of these customers' feet without measuring, and several by the sound of their walk before they've spoken.

"There's a man, comes in maybe twice a year, worked in construction his whole life, brings me the same pair of work boots since I don't know, 2003," Moyo says. "I've replaced every sole, every heel, restitched the uppers twice. At some point it's not really the original boot anymore, materially. But it's his boot. That's not something a new pair off a shelf can be, not for years."

The shop's survival, Moyo is careful to say, isn't a triumph over the economics working against it, it's a narrower business than his father ran, serving fewer customers who each spend more and expect more. He's not sure the shop survives him. His daughter works in medicine, has no interest in the trade, and he hasn't found an apprentice willing to commit the years it takes to learn stitching by hand at the level his own customers expect. For now, the machines still run, the leather still comes in on the delivery truck every Tuesday, and Fen Street still has one shop where a shoe, if it's built right, doesn't have to become trash just because it's worn out.
