The presses start at 11:40 p.m., and pressroom foreman Aiden Choudhury has learned to treat that number as fixed in a way almost nothing else in the building is. Editorial can hold a story until 11:15 for a breaking development. Advertising can swap a full-page ad until 11:20 in genuine emergencies. But 11:40 is when the plates go onto the press, and if that slips by more than a few minutes, the entire downstream chain, printing, binding inserts, loading trucks, and getting bundles to distribution points before dawn delivery routes begin, starts eating into a margin that doesn't have much slack to give.

"Everyone upstream of me thinks their deadline is the real deadline," Choudhury says, checking ink levels on the press's control panel. "Mine's the one that actually can't move, because after this, there's a truck schedule, and after the truck schedule, there's someone's driveway at six a.m. That whole chain only works backward from the truck leaving on time."

The plant runs four presses simultaneously for the paper's main regional edition, each printing a continuous web of paper at roughly fifteen meters per second once at full speed, a pace the press doesn't reach instantly. Startup itself takes close to ten minutes of gradual acceleration, checking color registration and print quality on early copies before the run is certified good and the crew stops pulling samples for inspection. A run producing this edition's roughly 40,000 copies takes just under an hour once at speed, plus the startup and a wind-down period for final bundling.

Paper supply is its own quiet crisis, managed nightly rather than solved once. Each roll weighs close to a ton and holds enough newsprint for a limited run of the press before it must be spliced, mid-run, to a fresh roll without stopping the machine, a task performed by an automated flying splice mechanism that the crew still watches closely, because a bad splice can tear the web and halt the entire press, a failure that costs precious minutes nobody in the schedule has budgeted for.

"A web break at one in the morning is the worst sound in this building," Choudhury says. "You hear it before you see it, and everyone just moves, because every minute the press is down is a minute we're not getting back before the trucks need to leave."

By 3 a.m., the run is typically complete and bundling machines have organized the papers into stacks by delivery route, a logistics operation nearly as precise as the printing itself, since a bundle destined for the wrong truck means a wrong or late paper somewhere on somebody's route. Trucks begin pulling out starting around 3:30, staggered by route distance, the farthest deliveries leaving first.

Choudhury has run the overnight pressroom for twelve years, long enough to have watched the industry around him contract considerably, fewer print subscribers, fewer competing papers, print runs a fraction of what they were when he started. He doesn't describe the job in terms of an industry he expects to outlast him. He describes it, instead, the way most people in this issue describe their own overnight work: as a window that has to be met precisely, every single night, regardless of what's true about the industry in the daylight hours nobody in his crew is ever awake to see.
