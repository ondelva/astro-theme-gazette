The diner has not locked its front door in the thirty-one years it's been open, a fact the owners advertise on a sign above the register that simply reads "Always." Line cook Teodor Marek has worked the overnight shift there for six years, from 11 p.m. to 7 a.m., four nights a week. We spoke with him during a lull between the bar crowd that thins out around 2:30 and the early shift workers who start arriving closer to 5.

**Who's actually in the diner at three in the morning?**

It changes completely depending on the hour, that's the part people don't expect. Right after the bars close, it's a loud crowd, mostly ordering big plates, wanting food fast, still on their night out. By two thirty, that clears out almost entirely and it goes quiet for maybe an hour, sometimes just one or two regulars, people who don't sleep well, or work nights themselves somewhere else and are winding down. Then closer to five, it flips again, guys heading to construction sites, hospital staff coming off or going onto shift, cab and delivery drivers. Three completely different rooms, same four walls, same night.

**Does the kitchen actually run differently across those shifts?**

Completely. The bar crowd wants it fast and doesn't care that much about precision, they want the plate in front of them. I can run a little looser, a little faster, on those orders. The quiet hour around three, that's when I actually do prep for the morning, cutting vegetables, portioning things out, because there's finally time. Then the five a.m. crowd, a lot of them are regulars who order the exact same thing every single day, and they notice immediately if it's not exactly right. That's actually the pickiest crowd of the whole night, not the drunk one.

**What's the hardest part of working overnight that people wouldn't guess?**

Staying sharp when nothing's happening. It's not the busy stretches that are hard, busy is easy, busy keeps you moving. It's three fifteen in the morning with one customer in the whole place and you have to stay alert enough to cook their order correctly and stay safe around the fryer and the grill, when your body is telling you as clearly as it can that you should be asleep. I've cut myself worse during a slow hour than during a rush, because a rush keeps your attention up and a slow hour lets it drift.

**Do you eat differently, live differently, because of the schedule?**

Everything shifts. My "dinner" is at midnight. My "breakfast" is at eight in the morning, after my shift, right before I try to sleep, which never quite makes sense to my body even after six years. I've missed most family things that happen on a normal schedule, most weddings, most Sunday dinners, because I'm either working or sleeping through them. My partner works days. We see each other for real maybe two, three hours a day, on purpose, scheduled like an appointment, which sounds sad when I say it out loud, but it's just what the job costs.

**Is there anything about the night shift you'd genuinely miss if you moved to days?**

The regulars, honestly. There's a particular kind of person who's a regular at three in the morning, and they're not like day customers. People talk more, or they don't talk at all and you respect that too, but there's an honesty to a three a.m. diner that a lunch rush doesn't have. Nobody's performing anything at that hour. People are just tired, or lonely, or working, or all three, and the diner's just a place that's open and doesn't ask why. I'd miss being that place for somebody. That part's not nothing.
