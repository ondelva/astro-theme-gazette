At half past midnight, the only light on the street comes from a bakery with its shutters half down and its ovens already at temperature. Inside, Antoine Rousseau is scaling dough into two-kilogram lumps on a floured bench, working from a schedule that hasn't meaningfully changed since his father ran the same ovens: mix by 1 a.m., first bake by 3:30, shop open at 7. Everything in between is a sequence timed so tightly that a fifteen-minute delay at any single step ripples through the rest of the night.

"People think baking is about the recipe," Rousseau says, without looking up from the bench. "The recipe is the easy part. Baking on a schedule, for a shop that opens at a fixed hour whether the dough is ready or not, that's the actual job."

## A night built around fermentation, not convenience

The night's structure is dictated almost entirely by the bread itself, specifically by fermentation, the process by which yeast and, for the bakery's sourdough lines, wild bacteria cultures slowly convert starches in the dough to gas and flavor compounds over hours, not minutes. Rousseau's baguette dough gets a cold, slow first rise, mixed the previous afternoon and held overnight at low temperature to develop flavor without the yeast activity racing out of control. What happens after midnight is the second phase: shaping, a shorter final proof, and baking in careful sequence so the ovens, which hold roughly forty loaves per load, are never sitting empty and never overloaded waiting for a batch that isn't ready.

### The night, in the order it happens

- **12:30 a.m.** Ovens up to temperature; the afternoon's baguette dough comes out of the cold room
- **1:00** Mixing for the day's second batch
- **2:15** Shaping and final proof
- **3:30** First bake, forty loaves to a load
- **5:00** Pastry line takes the ovens
- **6:45** Case filled; the bench that started the night gets cleaned

Sourdough runs on a different, longer clock entirely. The bakery maintains a starter culture, a living mixture of flour, water, and wild yeast and bacteria, that's been fed and maintained continuously for eleven years, since before Rousseau took over the shop from his father. Miss a feeding, let the culture go too long without fresh flour, and its fermentation activity weakens for days afterward, which is why someone, every single day without exception, feeds the starter regardless of holidays, illness, or how the rest of the night is going. "It doesn't take a day off because we're tired," Rousseau says. "It doesn't know we're tired."

## What the schedule costs

The bakery employs four people across the overnight shift: Rousseau, a second baker who handles the pastry line, and two apprentices who split shaping and oven work. All four work a schedule that puts them structurally out of sync with almost everyone they know outside the shop, sleeping through mid-morning into early afternoon, awake and functional for a handful of hours before the cycle starts again near midnight. Rousseau says the hardest adjustment wasn't physical, it was social: missing family dinners as a matter of routine, not exception, for over a decade.

## Three and a half hours from oven to counter

What the schedule buys, in exchange, is freshness measured in hours rather than a supply chain's usual measure of days. Bread baked at 3:30 a.m. and sold starting at 7 has had, at most, three and a half hours between oven and counter, a gap the shop treats as close to sacred; day-old bread is marked down and sold separately, not passed off as the morning's batch, a distinction Rousseau says some larger commercial bakeries in the area don't bother maintaining anymore.

By 6:45, the display case is full, the smell has replaced the pre-dawn quiet that filled the shop three hours earlier, and Rousseau starts the last task of his night: cleaning the bench where he scaled the first dough, before the day staff arrives to open the doors and the version of the bakery that customers actually see begins. He'll be home and asleep before most of the street outside has finished its first coffee.

"Everyone who eats the bread sees seven o'clock," he says. "Nobody sees midnight. That's fine. Midnight isn't for them. It's for the bread."
